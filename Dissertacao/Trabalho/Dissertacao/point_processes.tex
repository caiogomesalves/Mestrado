\section{Marked Spatio-Temporal Point Processes}
\label{sec-mark_point}

A spatio-temporal point process $\mathcal{X}$ is a sequence of random variables on $\{S \subset \mathds{R}^{2} \times [0,T]\}$ in which the number of points in $S \times T$ has a counting measure $N(S \times T)$ which is almost surely finite, when $S \times T$ is bounded. Usually $\mathcal{X}$ is observed in a spatio-temporal window $W = S \times T$, and a realization of $\mathcal{X}$ in $W$ is a set $\mathbf{x} = \{(s_{i},t_{i}), i = 1,2,\ldots,n\}$, where $s_{i} = (x_{i},y_{i})$ and $t_{i}$ represent the spatial coordinates and times of occurrence of the i-th event, respectively.

A marked spatio-temporal point process incorporates a uniquely associated component $m_{i}$ with each event of $\mathcal{X}$. In earthquake's catalogs, the marks $m_{i}$ usually denote the magnitude of each event, while $(s_{i},t_{i})$ represents the longitude/latitude of the earthquake's epicenter and the ordered times of occurrence.

Consider the event history $\mathcal{H}_{t} = \left\{(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i},m_{i}) \in \mathcal{X} : t_{i} < t\right\}$. A spatio-temporal point process can be fully characterized by it's conditional intensity function $\lambda(s,t | \mathcal{H}_{t})$ \cite{daley_jones_2003}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-intensity-function}
  \lambda(s,t|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = \lim_{\delta s,\delta t \to 0}\frac{\mathds{E}\left[N(\delta s \times \delta t)|\mathcal{H}_{t}\right]}{\delta s\, \delta t},
\end{equation}

\noindent that represents the expected number of events observed in an infinitesimal region of volume $(\delta s \times \delta t)$ centered around $(s, t)$, conditional on the observed history of the process up to time $t$. With it, we can define the process's associated compensator $\Lambda(t)$
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-compensator}
  \Lambda(t|\mathcal{H}_{u}) = \int_{0}^{t}\int_{S}\lambda(s,u|\mathcal{H}_{u})ds\,du,
\end{equation}

\noindent in which $N(s,t|\mathcal{H}_{t}) - \Lambda(t|\mathcal{H}_{t})$ is an local $\mathcal{H}_{t}$-martingale. The simplest and most common kind of spatio-temporal point process is the Poisson Process, defined as such:

\begin{definition}[Poisson Process]
  \label{def-poisson-process}
  A spatio-temporal counting process $\{N(s,t): s \in \mathds{R}^{2},t \in \mathds{R}_{+}\}$ is a Inhomogeneous Poisson Process with intensity function $\lambda(s,t)$ if:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item For any bounded window $W = (S \times T)$, $N(W) \sim \mathrm{Pois}\left(\int_{T}\int_{S}\lambda(s,t)ds \, dt\right)$;
  \item For any $n$ disjoint windows $W_{1}, W_{2}, \ldots, W_{n}$, the random variables $N(W_{1}), N(W_{2}), \ldots, N(W_{n})$ are mutually independent.
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

The Poisson Process has two properties that makes it very versatile for modeling point processes in general: \textbf{Superposition} and \textbf{Thinning}:

\begin{theorem}[Superposition of Poisson Processes]
  \label{teo-superposition}
  Given $k$ independent Poisson Processes $\mathcal{X}_{1}, \mathcal{X}_{2}, \ldots, \mathcal{X}_{k}$ with intensity functions $\lambda_{1}, \lambda_{2}, \ldots, \lambda_{k}$, the new process $\mathcal{X} = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{X}_{i}$ is a Poisson Process, with intensity function $\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Thinning of Poisson Processes]
  \label{teo-thinning}
  Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a Poisson Process with intensity function $\lambda$. For each occurrence of $\mathcal{X}$, assign it to process $\mathcal{X}_{1}$ with probability $p_{1}$, to process $\mathcal{X}_{2}$ with probability $p_{2}$, and so on up to process $\mathcal{X}_{k}$ with probability $p_{k}$, such that $\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i} = 1$. The new processes $\{\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{X}_{2},\ldots, \mathcal{X}_{k}\}$ are Poisson Processes with intensity functions $\{p_{1}\lambda, p_{2}\lambda, \ldots, p_{k}\lambda\}$, and are mutually independent.
\end{theorem}

With these two results, many point processes can have their intensity function modelled using Poisson Processes. However, one condition necessary for that is that $\lambda(s,t)$ must remain constant along time (i.e. $\lambda(s,t) = \lambda(s),\, \forall t \in [0,T]$), and in many cases that is not necessarily true.

\section{Hawkes Processes and the ETAS Model}
\label{sec-hawkes_proc}

In many point phenomena, the ocurrence of events increases the probability of further events occuring, such as the spread of diseases, urban violence (\citeonline{mohler_2011}, \citeonline{ludwig_2025}) and earthquakes. For these kinds of processes, the intensity function must change dynamically, in order to accommodate the increase in probability triggered by a previous event.

A self-exciting point process (occuring in time) was proposed by \citeonline{hawkes_1971a}, having intensity function given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-hawkes-uni}
  \lambda(t|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = \mu + \sum_{i:t_{i} < t}g(t - t_{i}),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu > 0$ is the baseline intensity of the process, and $\sum_{i:t_{i} < t}g(t - t_{i})$ represents the contribution of past events to the increase in the conditional intensity up to time $t$, with $g(v) \ge 0$ for nonnegative $v$ and 0 otherwise. To guarantee stationarity for this process (\citeonline{hawkes_1971b}), is necessary that $\int_{0}^{\infty}g(v)dv < 1$, otherwise the process can have infinite events in finite time.

The \textbf{ETAS} (\textit{Epidemic-Type Aftershock Sequence}) Model was proposed by \citeonline{ogata_1988}, and is the most used to describe earthquake occurrences. The initial model was only concerned with the event times and was a particular case of a marked point process, for it's inclusion of the earthquake's magnitudes. This model assumes that any earthquake can come from one of two sources: the \textbf{background} events come from a Inhomogeneous Poisson Process, independent of time, and the \textbf{offspring} were triggered by a previous event (either a background, or another offspring). Another way to call these events are \textbf{aftershocks}, and have a more geological interpretation.  \autoref{fig-branching-process} shows the branching structure of a temporal \textbf{ETAS}, with events being parents if they come from generation 0, and offsprings if they come from any other generation.

	\end{center}
\end{figure}

In a later work, \citeonline{ogata_1998} extended the ETAS model to accommodate the spatio-temporal distribution of earthquakes, in the form
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-mod-etas}
  \lambda_{\theta}(x,y,t|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = \mu(x,y) + \sum_{i: t_{i} < t}\kappa(m_{i})g(t - t_{i})f(x - x_{i}, y - y_{i};m_{i}),
\end{equation}

\noindent with it's components being:

\begin{itemize}
\item $\mu(x,y)$ is the background rate, representing a smooth function on the region $S$, with $\mu(x,y) > 0$. The parent events occur following this background rate, and it is assumed to be independent of time.
\item $\kappa(m_{i})$ is the productivity of the process, representing the expected number of events triggered from a event of magnitude $m_{i}$, taking form
  \begin{equation*}
    \kappa(m) = A\exp[\alpha(m - m_{0})],
  \end{equation*}
  with $A > 0$ and $\alpha > 0$ being unknown parameters, and $m_{0}$ the cutoff magnitude being considered in the study (i.e., all the $n$ events in the catalog have magnitudes $m_{i} \ge m_{0}, i = 1, 2,\ldots, n$).
\item $g(t - t_{i})$ is the probability density function of the time (in days) of a triggered event occuring at time $t_{i}$, and is assumed to be independent of the event magnitude $m_{i}$. A power law density is considered, based in the modified Omori's law (\citeonline{utsu_1995})
  \begin{equation*}
    g(t - t_{i}) =
    \begin{cases}
      (p - 1)c^{p-1}\left((t - t_{i}) + c\right)^{-p}, & t - t_{i} > 0 \\
      0, & t - t_{i} \le 0
    \end{cases},
  \end{equation*}
  with $c > 0$ and $p > 1$ being unknown parameters.
\item $f(x - x_{i}, y - y_{i};m_{i})$ is the probability density function of the location in space of a event triggered from a event occurred in $(x_{i}, y_{i})$ and magnitude $m_{i}$. The distances are considered to be in $(\text{degrees}\times\text{degrees})$ ($x$ is the longitude and $y$ is the latitude), and this can cause some considerable distortion in areas with big latitudes. An isotropic density function is considered
  \begin{equation*}
    f(x - x_{i}, y - y_{i};m_{i}) = \frac{q - 1}{\pi D \exp[\gamma(m_{i} - m_{0})]}\left(1 + \frac{(x - x_{i})^{2} + (y - y_{i})^{2}}{D \exp[\gamma(m_{i} - m_{0})]}\right)^{-q},
  \end{equation*}
  with $D > 0, \gamma > 0$ and $q > 1$ are unknown parameters.
\end{itemize}

Other parametrizations for $\lambda_{\theta}(x,y,t|\mathcal{H}_{t})$ were proposed by Ogata, but this one offers great flexibility by allowing the times of triggered events to be independent of the magnitudes, and by having separated parameters that control different aspects of the earthquake's intensity function behavior (temporal decay rate, influence of magnitudes in the number of expected offspring, spatial clustering, etc). The model can also incorporate the distribution of the marks/magnitudes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-model-etas-marks}
  \lambda_{\theta,\beta}(x,y,t,m|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = \nu(m)\lambda_{\theta}(x,y,t|\mathcal{H}_{t}),
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\nu(m)$ follows the Gutenberg-Richter distribution (\citeonline{gutenberg_1944})
\begin{equation*}
  \nu(m) = \beta\exp[-\beta(m - m_{0})], \,\, \beta > 0.
\end{equation*}

The expected number of offsprings generated from any event, regardless of its magnitude is given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-expect-triggered}
  e_{\mathrm{off}} = \int_{m_{0}}^{\infty}\kappa(m)\nu(m)dm = \frac{A\beta}{\beta - \alpha}.
\end{equation}

If $e_{\mathrm{off}} < 1$, the model is stable (\citeonline{daley_jones_2003}), and has cluster size of $\frac{1}{(1 - e_{\mathrm{off}})} = \frac{\beta - \alpha}{\beta(1 - A) - \alpha}$. Because the marks distribution is independent of the other parameters (as the complete intensity $\lambda_{\theta,\beta}(x,y,t,m|\mathcal{H}_{t})$ is the product of $\nu(v)$ and $\lambda_{\theta}(x,y,t|\mathcal{H}_{t})$), the estimation of $\beta$ can be done without the need to estimate the other parameters.

\section{Likelihood and Stochastic Declustering}
\label{sec-likelihood-etas}

Estimating the model parameters $\beta$ and $\theta = (A, \alpha, c, p, D, \gamma, q)$ and the background intensity function $\mu(x,y)$ is crucial to understand the seismological dynamic of a certain region, as it dictates when and where the aftershocks of an earthquake have more probability of happening. Moreover, early-warning systems depend on this estimates to make predictions in feasible time, as the time needed for these systems to mitigate further damages are between 1 and 10 minutes (\citeonline{allen_2019}).

For this, maximum likelihood methods for estimating the vector parameters have many desirable properties, such as being consistent, asymptotically unbiased and efficient (\citeonline{ogata_1978}). The ETAS model complete log-likelihood is given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-etas-loglik}
  l(\theta,\beta|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = \sum_{i = 1}^{n}\log(\lambda_{\theta,\beta}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i},m_{i}|\mathcal{H}_{t})) - \int_{m_{0}}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int\int_{S}\lambda_{\theta,\beta}(x,y,t,m|\mathcal{H}_{t})dx\, dy\, dt\, dm,
\end{equation}

\noindent where $T$ is the time of the last event in the catalog (the time integral starts at 0 because we can re-scale the event times $(t_{1},t_{2}, \ldots, t_{n})$ such that we have the fist event occur at time 0 with $(t_{1} - t_{1}, t_{2} - t_{1}, \ldots, t_{n} - t_{1}) = (0, t_{2}^{\ast}, \ldots, T)$). As seen in Equation; \eqref{eq-model-etas-marks}, $\beta$ and $\theta$ are separable, and $\int_{m_{0}}^{\infty}\nu(m)dm = 1$ (because it is a probability density function), we can decompose the previous log-likelihood as
\begin{equation*}
  l(\theta,\beta|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = l_{1}(\beta|\mathcal{H}_{t}) + l_{2}(\theta|\mathcal{H}_{t})
\end{equation*}

\noindent where
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-loglik-beta}
  l_{1}(\beta|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(\nu(m_{i})) = n\log(\beta) - \beta\sum_{i=1}^{n}(m_{i} - m_{0})
\end{equation}

\noindent and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-loglik-theta}
  l_{2}(\theta|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(\lambda_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i}|\mathcal{H}_{t})) - \int_{0}^{T}\int\int_{S}\lambda_{\theta}(x,y,t|\mathcal{H}_{t})dx\, dy\, dt.
\end{equation}

The maximum likelihood estimator of $\beta$ can be obtained analytically using Equation \eqref{eq-loglik-beta}
\begin{equation*}
  \hat{\beta} = \frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(m_{i} - m_{0})},
\end{equation*}

\noindent whereas the maximization of Equation \eqref{eq-loglik-theta} requires some form of numerical optimization method, to approximate the integral part. When we evaluate the integral term, we have
\begin{equation*}
  \int_{0}^{T}\int\int_{S}\lambda_{\theta}(x,y,t|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = T\int\int_{S}\mu(x,y) dx\, dy \, + \sum_{i:t_{i} < t}\kappa(m_{i})G(T - t_{i})F(S - s_{i};m_{i})
\end{equation*}

\noindent where $G(t)$ and $F(s)$ are the cumulative probability functions of $g(t)$ and $f(s)$, respectively, and are defined in \autoref{append-acum}. For any event not occurring near the spatio-temporal border (so that all the probability of being a offspring comes from the events inside the observed window, and not from any external event), we have that $G(T - t_{i}) \approx 1$ and $F(S - s_{i}) \approx 1$. This approximation simplifies the integral, making the log-likelihood easier to evaluate, when the assumptions are met.

However, the direct optimization of the complete log-likelihood remains complicated, due to two main factors: the $\log(\lambda_{\theta}(x_{i},y_{i},t_{i}|\mathcal{H}_{t}))$ term is the logarithm of a sum that has all the parameters and is not separable, and the computational cost for each evaluation is in the order of $\mathcal{O}(n^{2})$. If the underlying branching structure were known, the log-likelihood would be separable because the terms for the contribution of the background events and the offsprings would be either 1 or 0 for any occurrence.

Consider that each event $j$ has a parent $u_{j} \in \{0,1,\ldots,j-1\}$, in which $u_{j} = 0$ indicates a event that has come from the background rate function. If $u_{j}$ were observed, we would have
\begin{align}
  \begin{split}
  \label{eq-sep-loglik}
    l_{2}(\theta|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = &\sum_{j = 1}^{n}\left[\mathds{1}(u_{j} = 0)\log(\mu(x_{j},y_{j})) + \sum_{i < j}\mathds{1}(u_{j} \neq 0)\log(\kappa(m_{i})g(t_{j}-t_{i})f(s_{j} - s_{i};m_{i}))\right] - \\
                                    &T\int\int_{S}\mu(x,y)dx\, dy + \sum_{i:t_{i} < t}\kappa(m_{i})G(T - t_{i})F(S - s_{i};m_{i}),
\end{split}
\end{align}

\noindent making the log-likelihood separable, and with direct evaluation of the parameters. \citeonline{zhuang_2002} proposed a stochastic declustering method based on the decomposition of the conditional intensity in the ratio between the total intensity and the local intensity. Denote $\rho_{j}$ as the probability that the event $j$ is an background event, and $\rho_{ij}$ the probability that event $j$ was triggered by event $i$. Then, we have
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-rho-j}
  \rho_{ij} = \frac{\kappa(m_{i})g(t_{j} - t_{i})f(s_{j} - s_{i};m_{i})}{\lambda_{\theta}(x_{j},y_{j},t_{j}|\mathcal{H}_{t_{j}})},
\end{equation}

\noindent and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-rho-ij}
  \rho_{j} = \frac{\mu(x_{j},y_{j})}{\lambda_{\theta}(x_{j},y_{j},t_{j}|\mathcal{H}_{t_{j}})} = 1 - \sum_{i<j}\rho_{ij}.
\end{equation}

Thus, the correct estimation of $\theta$ should in turn help estimate whether an earthquake was triggered by the background rate, or if it is an aftershock of another previous event in the catalog. When all the events in the catalog are far from the spatio-temporal border of the observed window $W$, we can consider the approximation $G(T - t_{i}) = F(S - s_{i};m_{i}) \approx 1$, and with the results from Equations \eqref{eq-rho-j} and \eqref{eq-rho-ij}, we can substitute the indicator function for the unobserved $u_{j}$'s in Equation \eqref{eq-sep-loglik} for their estimated probabilities as such
\begin{align}
  \begin{split}
    \label{eq-loglik-simp}
    l_{2}(\theta|\mathcal{H}_{t}) = &\sum_{j = 1}^{n}\rho_{j}\log\Big(\mu(x_{j},y_{j})\Big) + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij}\log\Big(\kappa(m_{i})g(t_{j} - t_{i})f(s_{j} - s_{i};m_{i})\Big) - \\
                                    &T\int\int_{S}\mu(x,y)dx\, dy + \sum_{i:t_{i} < t}\kappa(m_{i}).
  \end{split}
\end{align}

\section{EM-Type Algorithm}
\label{sec-em-type-alg}

For the estimation of the parameter vector $\theta$, \citeonline{veen_2008} propose an EM-type approach, in which the probabilities $\rho_{j}$ are updated in the E-step and the simplified complete log-likelihood (as shown in Equation \eqref{eq-loglik-simp}) is maximized in the M-step. Since the $\beta$ parameter, who controls the distribution of the magnitudes, can be estimated analytically without interfering in the estimation of $\theta$, it will be not included in the EM scheme.

Consider a catalog containing $n$ earthquakes above a specified cutoff magnitude $m_{0}$, arranged by event times, and for each event $i \in \{1,2,\ldots,n\}$, the catalog contains their longitudes and latitudes, magnitudes and time (in days) since the start of the monitoring $T_{0}$ (as presented in \autoref{tab-catalog-example}). Let $\Delta t_{ij} = (t_{j} - t_{i}), i<j,$ and $\Delta m_{i} = (m_{i} - m_{0})$.

To initiate the algorithm, an initial guess for $\theta$ must be given, as well as an initial guess for $\mu(x,y)$ in all the study region. In their work, \citeonline{veen_2008} separate $S$ in arbitrary blocks and treat $\mu(x,y)$ as different constants in each block, but for this work, we consider it to be a continuous function in $S$. For the initial guess, we assume that the background rate function is constant in all the region, in a way that $\mu^{(0)}(x,y) = \frac{n}{T|S|}$ , where $n$ is the number of events and $|S|$ is the size of the region (this initial guess consider that all events are background, making $\rho_{j} = 1$ for all $j$ and consequently $\sum_{j}\rho_{j} = n$). This is evaluated in a rectangular grid along $S$, in which each cell receives the exact mass value of the intensity function inside it.

In \citeonline{jalilian_2019}, the author cautions that when using the \texttt{etasfit()} function from the package \texttt{ETAS}, the algorithm used for the parameter estimation is very sensitive for the initial guess of $\theta^{(0)}$, to the point of warning the user when running the function. The sensitivity to the initial guess of the proposed EM-type algorithm will be further explored in \autoref{sec-sensiv-theta-0}.

\subsection{E-Step}
\label{subsec-e-step}

The estimation of the probabilities that event $i$ triggered event $j$ in the iteration $k$ is done using the expression given by Equation \eqref{eq-rho-ij}, considering $(\theta^{(k)},\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}(x,y))$, where $\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}(x_{j},y_{j})$ is the value of $\hat{\mu}^{(k)}(x,y)$ evaluated in the coordinates of event $j$. To ensure the background rate is estimated continuously, a smoothing step is included after the maximization step. For it, we consider a adaptative bandwidth isotropic gaussian kernel density estimator given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-kernel-smoothing}
  K_{h_{j}}(u) = \frac{1}{2\pi h_{j}^{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{|u|^{2}}{2h_{j}^{2}}\right\},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $|u|$ is the size of $u \in \mathds{R}^{2}$, $h_{j} = \max\left(d_{j}^{(n_{p})}, d_{\text{min}}\right)$ is the adaptative bandwidth size around the $j$-th event and $c_{j} = \int_{S}K_{h_{j}}(u - (x_{j},y_{j}))\, du$ is the mass of the kernel $j$ in the observed window. The value of $h_{j}$ is given by the maximum between some designated minimum distance $d_{\text{min}}$, and the distance from that event to it's $n_{p}$-th nearest neighbor. This form is proposed by \citeonline{zhuang_2002}, and in it the authors find that the values for $n_{p}$ and $d_{\text{min}}$ are not sensitive and do not interfere significantly in the final estimate of $\mu(x,y)$, when their values remains in the ranges 15-100 for $n_{p}$ and 0.01-0.1 for $d_{\text{min}}$. We adopt the values $n_{p} = 20$ and $d_{\text{min}} = 0.02$ as suggested by the authors.

Each kernel integrates 1 in $\mathds{R}^{2}$, so $T\int\int_{S}\mu(x,y)dx\,dy$ is equal to the sum of $\rho_{j}$, except the mass that escapes the study region $S$. To correct this, the value of $c_{j}$ is calculated in such a way that each kernel divided by $c_{j}$ integrates 1 in the observed window $S$, and because of this, the value of $T \int\int_{S}\mu(x,y)dx\,dy$ is exactly $\sum_{j}\rho_{j}$. For a rectangular window with limits $[X_{0},X_{1}]$ and $[Y_{0},Y_{1}]$, the value of $c_{j}$ has closed form, given by the product of cumulative standard Normal distributions
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-c-j}
  c_{j} = \left[\Phi\left(\frac{X_{1} - x_{j}}{h_{j}}\right) - \Phi\left(\frac{X_{0} - x_{j}}{h_{j}}\right)\right]\left[\Phi\left(\frac{Y_{1} - y_{j}}{h_{j}}\right) - \Phi\left(\frac{Y_{0} - y_{j}}{h_{j}}\right)\right].
\end{equation}

Using this, each kernel has weight $w_{j} = \frac{\rho_{j}}{T\,c_{j}}$. Each event $j$ contributes to $\hat{\mu}(x,y)$ with $\frac{\rho_{j}}{2\pi Th_{j}^{2}}$, and if $h_{j}$ is small (as the cases in which $h_{j} = d_{\text{min}}$) this contribution may exceed the value of $\hat{\mu}$ and feeds back $\rho_{j}$ in the next iteration. To mitigate this, we consider a leave-one-out scheme for the estimation of $\tilde{\mu}(x_{j},y_{j})$, which is given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-tilde-mu}
  \begin{split}
    \tilde\mu_{j}^{(k + 1)} &= \sum_{l \neq j}w_l^{(k)}K_{h_{l}}\left((x_{j}, y_{j}) - (x_{l}, y_{l})\right), \\
                            &= \frac{1}{T}\sum_{l \neq j}\frac{\rho_{l}^{(k)}}{c_{l}}\frac{1}{2\pi h_{l}^{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(x_{j} - x_{l})^{2} + (y_{j} - y_{l})^{2}}{2h_{l}^{2}}\right\}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

In the $k$-th iteration of the algorithm, the E-Step uses $\tilde{\mu}^{(k)}(x,y)$ calculated at the end of the previous iteration using $\rho^{(k - 1)}$, and the value for the conditional intensity function is calculated as
\begin{equation*}
    \lambda_{j}^{(k)} = \tilde{\mu}_{j}^{(k)}(x_{j},y_{j}) + \sum_{i<j}\kappa(m_{i})g(t_{j} - t_{i})f(x_{j} - x_{i}, y_{j} - y_{i}; m_{i}),
\end{equation*}

\noindent and it is used to calculate $\rho_{ij}^{(k)}$ and $\rho_{j}^{(k)}$ as
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
    \rho_{j}^{(k)} &= \frac{\tilde{\mu}_{j}^{(k)}(x_{j}, y_{j})}{\lambda_{j}^{(k)}}, \\
    \rho_{ij}^{(k)} &= \frac{\kappa(m_{i})g(t_{j} - t_{i})f(x_{j} - x_{i}, y_{j} - y_{i}; m_{i})}{\lambda_{j}^{(k)}}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation*}

These values are then provided for the M-Step as the latent probabilities that event $j$ is a background event. In the first iteration (when $k = 0$) the value for $\tilde{\mu}^{(0)}(x_{j},y_{j})$ is $\frac{n}{T|S|}$ for all events.

\subsection{M-Step}
\label{subsec-m-step}

The $Q$ function to be maximized (considering the approximation $G(T - t_{i}) = F(S - s_{i}) = 1$) is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \label{eq-q-func}
    Q(\theta,\mu|\theta^{(k)},\mu^{(k)}) = &\underbrace{\sum_{j}\rho_{j}\log\left(\mu^{(k)}(x_{j},y_{j})\right) - T \int\int_{S}\mu^{(k)}(x,y)dx\, dy}_{Q_{\mu}} + \\
                                           &\underbrace{\sum_{j}\sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij}\log\left(\kappa(m_{i})g(\Delta t_{ij})f(s_{j} - s_{i};m_{i})\right) - \sum_{i}\kappa(m_{i})}_{Q_{\theta}}.
  \end{split}
\end{equation}

The logarithmic part of the $Q_{\theta}$ function can be separated into independent blocks that depend only on some of the parameters, due to the parametrization of $\lambda_{\theta}$, as such
\begin{equation*}
  l_{Q_{\theta}} = \log(\kappa(m_{i})g(\Delta t_{ij})f(s_{j} - s_{i};m_{i})) = &\underbrace{\log(\kappa(m_{i}))}_{A,\alpha} + \underbrace{\log(g(\Delta t_{ij}))}_{c,p} + \underbrace{\log(f(s_{j} - s_{i};m_{i}))}_{D,\gamma,q},
\end{equation*}

\noindent where $A$ and $\alpha$ are responsible for the productivity, $c$ and $p$ for the temporal aspects of the process and $D$, $\gamma$ and $q$ for the spatial distribution. This makes the derivatives in relation to each variable in $\theta$ to be dependent only of itself and the other variables in it's block, in a way that the parameters only interfere on the aspects they are supposed to control. If approximation methods for the gradient based in finite differences are used, the resulting hessian matrix will have crossed terms close to 0, but not really 0.

\subsubsection{Analytical Gradient for $Q$}
\label{subsubsec-gradient-q}

In most computational maximization routines based in Newton/quasi-Newton methods, providing the gradient of the objective function eliminates the need to estimate it by finite differences methods, improving significantly the computational time necessary for the convergence of the method.

Let $r_{ij}^{2} = \left[(x_{j} - x_{i})^{2} + (y_{j} - y_{i})^{2}\right]$, $S_{i} = D\exp(\gamma\Delta m_{i})$, $V_{ij} = \frac{r_{ij}^{2}}{S_{i}}$, $U_{ij} = 1 + V_{ij}$ and $W_{ij} = q\frac{V_{ij}}{U_{ij}} - 1$, for any events $i$ and $j$ such that $i < j$. With these expressions, the derivatives of $l_{Q_{\theta}}$ with relation to each variable are
\begin{align}
  \label{eq-grad-lq}
    &\frac{\partial l_{Q_{\theta}}}{\partial A} = \frac{1}{A}, &\frac{\partial l_{Q_{\theta}}}{\partial \alpha} &= \Delta m_{i}, \notag \\
    &\frac{\partial l_{Q_{\theta}}}{\partial c} = \frac{p - 1}{c} - \frac{p}{\Delta t_{ij} + c}, &\frac{\partial l_{Q_{\theta}}}{\partial p} &= \frac{1}{p - 1} + \log\left(\frac{c}{\Delta t_{ij} + c}\right), \notag \\
    &\frac{\partial l_{Q_{\theta}}}{\partial D} = \frac{W_{ij}}{D}, &\frac{\partial l_{Q_{\theta}}}{\partial \gamma} &= \Delta m_{i}W_{ij}, \notag \\
  &\frac{\partial l_{Q_{\theta}}}{\partial q} = \frac{1}{q- 1} - \log(U_{ij}).
\end{align}

The compensator term for $Q_{\theta}$, given by $\sum_{i}\kappa(m_{i})$, depends only of $A$ and $\alpha$, and has derivatives given by
\begin{align}
  \label{eq-grad-comp}
    &\frac{\partial}{\partial A}\sum_{i}\kappa(m_{i}) = \sum_{i}\exp(\alpha\Delta m_{i}), &\frac{\partial}{\partial \alpha}\sum_{i}\kappa(m_{i}) = \sum_{i}A\Delta m_{i}\exp(\alpha\Delta m_{i}).
\end{align}

When we combine the expressions in Equations \eqref{eq-grad-lq} and \eqref{eq-grad-comp}, with the probabilities calculated in the E-Step $\rho_{ij}$, we have an complete expresion for the gradient of the $Q_{\theta}$ function, given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-grad-q}
  \begin{split}
    \frac{\partial Q_{\theta}}{\partial A} &= \frac{1}{A}\sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij} - \sum_{i}\exp(\alpha\Delta m_{i}), \\
    \frac{\partial Q_{\theta}}{\partial \alpha} &= \sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij}\Delta m_{i} - A\sum_{i}\Delta m_{i}\exp(\alpha\Delta m_{i}), \\
    \frac{\partial Q_{\theta}}{\partial c} &= \sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij}\left(\frac{p - 1}{c} - \frac{p}{\Delta t_{ij} + c}\right), \\
    \frac{\partial Q_{\theta}}{\partial p} &= \sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij}\left(\frac{1}{p - 1} + \log\left(\frac{c}{\Delta t_{ij} + c}\right)\right), \\
    \frac{\partial Q_{\theta}}{\partial D} &= \frac{1}{D}\sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij}W_{ij}, \\
    \frac{\partial Q_{\theta}}{\partial \gamma} &= \sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij}\Delta m_{i}W_{ij}, \\
    \frac{\partial Q_{\theta}}{\partial q} &= \sum_{i < j}\rho_{ij}\left(\frac{1}{q - 1} - \log(U_{ij})\right).
  \end{split}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Smoothing Step}
\label{subsubsec-smoothing}

The unrestricted maximizer for $Q_{\mu}$ is the sum of the pontual masses with weights $\frac{\rho_{j}}{T}$ in each of the cells in the study region, becoming degenerated and thus needing some form of smoothing. For that, we use the adaptative bandwidth gaussian kernel density estimator (KDE) for $\mu(x,y)$, given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-smoothing-mu}
  \hat\mu^{(k+1)}(x,y)=\sum_{j = 1}^{n}w_{j}^{(k)}K_{h_{j}}\left((x,y)-(x_{j},y_{j})\right),
\end{equation}

Given some fixed $\mu(x,y)$, the M-step in $\theta$ is a real M-step in the sense that if $Q\left(\theta^{(k + 1)},\mu^{(k)}|\theta^{(k)},\mu^{(k)}\right) \ge Q\left(\theta^{(k)},\mu^{(k)}|\theta^{(k)},\mu^{(k)}\right)$, then the observed log-likelihood does not decrease (see \autoref{append-non-decreasing}). However, this smoothing step does not maximize $Q_{\mu}$, so the complete scheme is an EM-algorithm with a smoothing step along the lines of the EMS algorithm (\citeonline{silverman_1990}), with no guarantee for joint monotonicity.

\section{Quantification of Uncertainty}
\label{sec-uncertainty}

At the end of the M-Step, the hessian matrix is the information of the complete data, conditional in the estimated probabilities $\rho_{j}$. According to \citeonline{louis_1982}, the observed information underestimates the uncertainty, because it does not take in consideration the loss in information the estimation of the latent quantities cause. Using the author's method, we can subtract the information lost by the latent quantities using
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-inf-obser}
  I_{\text{obs}}(\hat{\theta}) = \mathds{E}\left[- \nabla^{2}\log(L_{c})\, | \, \text{data}\right] - \text{Var}\left[\nabla\log(L_{c})\, | \,\text{data}\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathds{E}\left[- \nabla^{2}\log(L_{c})\, | \, \text{data}\right] = -\nabla^{2}Q$, the hessian matrix returned at the end of the M-Step. Conditional in the data, the variables $u_{j}$ are independent, with $P(u_{j} = 0) = \rho_{j}$ and $P(u_{j} = i) = \rho_{ij}$. Let $\pi_{ji}$ be that probability and $s_{jk}$ the score of the k-th term of the event $j$. The variance term of the observed information become
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-var-latent}
  \mathrm{Var}\big[\nabla\log L_c | \text{data}\big] = \sum_{j}\left[\sum_{k}\pi_{jk}\,s_{jk}s_{jk}^{\top} - \left(\sum_{k}\pi_{jk}s_{jk}\right)\left(\sum_{k}\pi_{jk}s_{jk}\right)^{\top}\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent which is a pairwise sum that can be calculated using the gradients given by Equation \eqref{eq-grad-q}. The estimated covariance matrix is the inverse of the observed information matrix, and the standard errors for $\hat{\theta}$ is the square root of the diagonal of the inversed information matrix, and are conditional in $\hat{\mu}(x,y)$, $\hat{b}$ and $M_{0}$.

Two other strategies to estimate the standard errors can be used: the first one is to consider directly the hessian matrix returned by the M-Step, calculated using the analytical gradient of $Q$ and some finite differences method. This underestimates the variability of $\hat{\theta}$, but is less noticeable as the number of observations grow, making the estimation of the errors for big catalogs more reliable and direct. The other is to simulate $B$ catalogs using the estimated values of $\hat{\theta}$ and $\hat{\mu}(x,y)$, use them to estimate again the parameters, and using these bootstrap estimates to estimate the empirical standard errors and percentiles.

\section{Computational Implementation}
\label{sec-comput-impl}

The proposed method was implemented in the R programming language, version 4.6.1 (\citeonline{R_lang}). The maximization step uses the \texttt{optim()} function supplied with the analytical gradient in the argument \texttt{gr}. The default option for \texttt{optim()} is to minimize the target function, so the implementation returns the value of $-Q(\theta,\mu|\theta^{(k)},\mu^{(k)})$, and it's minimization is equivalent to the maximization of $Q(\theta, \mu|\theta^{(k)}, \mu^{(k)})$.

As the parametric space of $\theta$ have restrictions ($A, \alpha, c, D, \gamma$ must be greater than 0, and $p, q$ must be greater than 1), the optimization method chosen was the L-BFGS-B (Limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno with Box constraints), as it uses the analytical gradient of the objective function, and allows the imposition of box constraints that restrict the search space. In the \texttt{ETAS} package there are no restrictions to the search space, and in some cases the algorithm can give estimates outside the possible range, making so that the optimization never stops and needing to terminate the process manually.

The EMS algorithm has a compound stopping criterion, wich evaluates a relative plus absolute change in both $\hat{\mu}^{(k)}(x,y)$ and $\hat{\theta}^{(k)}$ that preserves the difference in scale that the parameters may have, verifying if $|\hat{\mu}^{(k+1)}(x,y) - \hat{\mu}^{(k)}(x,y)| < \epsilon_{\mu}$ and $|\hat{\theta}^{(k+1)} - \hat{\theta}^{(k)}| < \epsilon_{\theta}$, with $\epsilon_{\theta} = \epsilon_{\mathrm{abs}} + \epsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}|\theta^{(k)}|$ where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}$ and $\epsilon_{\mathrm{rel}}$ are the absolute and relative tolerances set. If both criteria have been met, the algorithm stops (with a safeguard of at maximum 100 iterations). The implementation uses the \texttt{parallel} package to parallelize the E-Step, in which it automatically allocates the computations to a designated number of cores to be processed simultaneously. It's worth noting that this is done only in Linux machines, and does not work natively in Windows.

\section{Residual Analysis and Goodness of Fit}
\label{sec-residue-analysis}

To evaluate the quality of the estimated quantities $\hat{\theta}$ and $\hat{\mu}(x,y)$, we use residue analysis for the temporal and spatial aspects of the ETAS model.

\subsection{Temporal Residues}
\label{subsec-temporal-resid}

The estimated compensator for the process is given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-est-compensator}
  \hat{\Lambda}(t) = \frac{t}{T}\sum_{j}\rho_{j} + \sum_{i: t_{i} < t}\kappa(m_{i})G(t - t_{i})F(S - s_{i};m_{i}),
\end{equation}

\noindent and with it, we resort to the random time change theorem, defined as such:

\begin{theorem}[Random time change]
  \label{teo-mudanca-tempo}
  Let $0 < t_{1} < t_{2} < \ldots < t_{n}$ be the realization of a Point Process with conditional intensity function $\lambda(t|\mathcal{H}_{t})$, where $\lambda(t|\mathcal{H}_{t}) > 0$ for all $t \in [0,T]$. Then, the transformed times:
  \begin{equation*}
    \{\Lambda(t_{1}), \Lambda(t_{2}), \ldots, \Lambda(t_{n})\} = \{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{n}\},
  \end{equation*}
  are the points of a unidimensional unit rate Poisson Process, if $\Lambda(t|\mathcal{H}_{t})$ is almost surely finite for all $t \in [0,T]$.
\end{theorem}

This theorem holds for spatio-temporal point processes, and can be used to verify if $\hat{c}$ and $\hat{p}$ have been well estimated. Let $\tau_{j} = \hat{\Lambda}(t_{j})$ be the transformed times of the catalog, then
\begin{equation*}
U_{j} = 1 - \exp\left\{-(\tau_{j} - \tau_{j - 1})\right\},
\end{equation*}

\noindent are independent and identically distributed random variables, with $U_{j} \sim U(0,1)$. With this, we can use classical methods to verify goodness of fit, such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and graphical validation methods (such as if the graphic of $N(\tau)$ versus $\tau$ is near the identity line, or if the QQ-plot of $U_{j}$ is well behaved). For the evaluation in intervals, we can consider the temporal Pearson residuals. Let $I_{b} = [t_{b}^{-}, t_{b}^{+}], \, t_{b}^{-} < t_{b}^{+}$ be a temporal interval, with the observed number of events in $I_{b}$ to be $O_{b} = N(I_{b})$ (the counting of events in which $t_{j} \in I_{b}$), and the expected number of events in $I_{b}$ to be $E_{b} = \hat{\Lambda}(t_{b}^{+}) - \hat{\Lambda}(t_{b}^{-})$. Then, the temporal Pearson residuals for the interval $I_{b}$ are given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-pearson-resid}
  R_{b} = \frac{O_{b} - E_{b}}{\sqrt{E_{b}}}.
\end{equation}

With them, we can see if there are time periods with poor adjustment, in which the values for the residues become faily big (usually, $R_{b} > 2$ is considered to be an indication of a poor fit). For these methods to be applicable, the times of events must be in chronological order, and we must choose an viable size for the intervals, because temporal windows in which $E_{b}$ is faily small will have unstable Pearson residuals.

\subsection{Spatial Residues}
\label{subsec-spatial-resid}

For the spatial residues, we calculate the expected spatial density, in events by area unit along $[0,T]$, given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-expec-spatial-dens}
  \hat{\lambda}_{E}(x,y) = T\hat{\mu}(x,y) + \sum_{i}\kappa(m_{i})G(T - t_{i})f(x - x_{i}, y - y_{i};m_{i}),
\end{equation}

\noindent and is used in the smoothed residuals, comparing the observed and expected values in the same spatial kernel $K_{h}$
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-smooth-spatial-resid}
  \hat{s}_{h}(x,y) = \sum_{j}K_{h}((x,y) - (x_{j},y_{j})) - \int\int_{S}K_{h}((x,y) - u)\hat{\lambda}_{E}(u)du.
\end{equation}

For the evaluation in a rectangular grid formed by pixels with limits $(x^{-},y^{-})$ and $(x^{+}, y^{+})$, we can resort to spatial Pearson residuals defined in Equation \eqref{eq-pearson-resid}, where $O_{b}$ are the counting of events that occurred inside the pixel (i.e., $O_{b} = \sum_{i}\mathds{1}(x_{i} \in [x^{-},x^{+}] \cup y_{i} \in [y^{-}, y^{+}])$), and $E_{b} = \int_{y^{-}}^{y^{+}}\int_{x^{-}}^{x^{+}}\hat{\lambda}_{E}(x,y)dx\, dy$. With them, we can evaluate if some region has problems with the estimation, mainly from the background rate. Another forms of residual analysis such as thinning residuals and Voronoi residuals can be used, in cases where the objective region is not rectangular.

\section{Catalog Simulation}
\label{sec-cat-simulation}

In order to investigate whether the proposed method is well suited for the fit of the ETAS model, we must be able to control different aspects of some catalogs, and for that a catalog simulator must be constructed. We consider a ramification scheme, where the background events are simulated first, and the offspring are simulated sequentially for every event previously in the catalog.

First, provided the real values for $\theta$ and the function $\mu(x,y)$, the number of parents $N_{0}$ is simulated considering that $N_{0} \sim \mathrm{Pois}\left(T\int\int_{S}\mu(x,y)dx\, dy\right)$, with times uniformly distributed in $(0,T)$, and locations simulated from the Inhomogeneous Poisson Process with intensity function $\mu(x,y)$. The magnitudes follow the Gutenberg-Richter law, with truncation in $M_{\text{max}}$ in order to not generate earthquakes above some limit value (for example, 0.1 degrees above the maximum magnitude in the catalog), defined as such
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-sim-magnitudes}
  m = m_{0} - \frac{1}{\beta}\log\left[1 - u_{m}(1 - \exp(- \beta(M_{\text{max}} - m_{0})))\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where $u_{m} \sim U(0,1)$. Then, the offspring for each event $i$ in generation 0 generates $N_{i}$ offsprings, with $N_{i} \sim \mathrm{Pois}(\kappa(m_{i}))$, times given by $t = t_{i} + c\left[(1 - u_{t})^{-\frac{1}{p - 1}} - 1\right]$, and spatial dislocation with radius $r = \sqrt{S_{i}\left[(1 - u_{r})^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}-1\right]}$ and angle $\phi \sim U(0,2\pi)$, with $u_{t},u_{r} \sim U(0,1)$. If the event simulated time is bigger than the maximum time $T$ or if the simulated location lies outside the target region $S$, the point is discarded. All these events are then added to the catalog, and the process repeats for all the generations. If there are no more generations or if there are no more events with times $t < T$, the algorithm stops and the catalog is complete.

With this scheme we are able to control all the aspects of the seismological activity of some region. The expected number of events is given by
\begin{equation*}
  \mathds{E}(N) \approx \frac{T \int \int_{S}\mu(x,y)dx\, dy}{1 - e_{\mathrm{off}}},
\end{equation*}

\noindent where $e_{\mathrm{off}} = \frac{A\beta}{\beta - \alpha}$ is the expected number of offspring generated from any event, as defined in Equation \eqref{eq-expect-triggered}.

With the proposed method well defined (which includes the EMS algorithm, the analytical gradient of the $Q$ function, the quantification of the uncertainty and the means to diagnose the quality of fit) and the simulator that permits testing the model under known circumstances, we can explore how the model performs under different scenarios.

\part{Results}
\label{part-results}

\chapter{Simulation Studies}
\label{chap-simulation-studies}

For the simulation studies, three main questions were explored: can the proposed method recover the real parameters used for the simulation, is the method sensitive to changes in the initial guesses for $\theta^{(0)}$ and $\mu^{(0)}(x,y)$, and how the estimated values behave as the size of the catalogs increase (and does the estimation take a feasible time to be computed)?

All the simulations consider some characteristics who are common between all the experiments: the spatial window $W$ is the square in $[0,8] \times [0,8]$, the background rate function is the rescalated sum of radial exponentials with a floor, such that the integral of the $\mu(x,y)$ in the window to be $\mu_{\mathrm{tot}}$ events/day. The $\mu(x,y)$ function is given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-mu-simulado}
  \mu(x,y) = s\left[\epsilon + \sum_{k = 1}^{K}a_{k}\exp\left(- \frac{||(x,y) - (x_{k},y_{k})||}{l_{k}}\right)\right],
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\epsilon$ is the floor value that controls the minimal intensity, $k = 1,2,\ldots,K$ is the number of center points of the radial exponentials, $a_{k}$ are the amplitudes and $l_{k}$ the scale of such exponentials and $s$ is the scaling factor, given by
\begin{equation*}
  s = \frac{\mu_{\mathrm{tot}}}{\int\int_{W}\left[\epsilon + \sum_{k = 1}^{K}a_{k}\exp\left(- \frac{||(x,y) - (x_{k},y_{k})||}{l_{k}}\right)\right]dx\, dy}.
\end{equation*}

For this study, we consider $K = 2$, with coordinates $(2.5,5)$ and $(5.5,2)$, amplitudes $a_{1} = 1$ and $a_{2} = 0.6$, scales $l_{1} = 0.8$ and $l_{2} = 1.2$, $\mu_{\mathrm{tot}} = 0.11$ and $\epsilon = 0.05$. The true background rate used for the simulations is presented in \autoref{fig-true-background}. The true value for the parameters is $\theta = (A = 0.5, \alpha = 1.5, c = 0.25, p = 1.5, D = 0.1, q = 2, \gamma = 1)$, the threshold magnitude is $m_{0} = 4.5$, the $b$ value used for the simulation of the magnitudes was $b = 1.5$, and consequently, $\beta = b\log(10) \approx 3.45$. With that, we can calculate the $e_{\mathrm{off}}$ ratio and see that $e_{\mathrm{off}} = 0.8839 < 1$, making the process be subcritical.

\begin{figure}[tb]
	\caption{\label{fig-true-background}True background rate used in simulations.}
	\begin{center}	    \includegraphics[scale=1]{Images/real_background.pdf}
	\end{center}
\end{figure}

If we want to achieve a certain number of observations $n_{\mathrm{targ}}$, the time period chosen to construct the catalogs must be choose considering that $\mathds{E}(N) \approx \frac{T\mu_{\mathrm{tot}}}{(1 - e_{\mathrm{off}})} \rightarrow T \approx \frac{n_{\mathrm{targ}}(1 - e_{\mathrm{off}})}{\mu_{\mathrm{tot}}}$. The EMS algorithm stopping criterion was set to a relative change in the order $10^{-3}$ and an absolute change of $10^{-6}$, both in the grid cells that evaluate $\hat{\mu}(x,y)$ and in $\hat{\theta}$.

Consider $B$ different fits, the metrics used for evaluating $\hat{\theta}$ are the bias, RMSE (square root of mean squared error) and the coverage, given by
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-metrics-theta}
  \begin{split}
    \mathrm{Bias}_{l} &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b}\hat{\theta}_{l}^{(b)} - \theta_{l},\\
    \mathrm{RSME}_{l} &= \sqrt{\mathrm{Bias}_{l}^{2} + \widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat{\theta}_{l})}, \\
    \mathrm{Cover}_{l} &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b}\mathds{1}\left\{|\hat{\theta}_{l}^{(b)} - \theta_{l}| \le 1.96\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{l}^{(b)}\right\},
  \end{split}
\end{equation}

\noindent and the metrics used to evaluate $\hat{\mu}(x,y)$ are the L1 difference and the Brier Score, defined as

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq-metrics-mu}
  \begin{split}
    L_{1} &= \frac{\int\int_{W}|\hat{\mu}(x,y) - \mu(x,y)|dx\, dy}{\int\int_{W}\mu(x,y)dx\, dy}, \\
    \mathrm{Brier} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j = 1}^{N}\left(\hat{\rho}_{j} - \mathds{1}\left\{u_{j} = 0\right\}\right).
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
